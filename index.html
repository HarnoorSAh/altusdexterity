Robotic hands that work the way a surgeon’s do

Altus Dexterity is building a two-armed surgical robot with soft, humanlike hands that pick up ordinary surgical instruments, handle delicate tissue, and take direction from the surgeon by voice and gesture.

When surgeons are needed most, there often aren’t enough of them

Mass casualty events, natural disasters, battlefields and remote locations all share one problem: more patients than trained hands to treat them.

The DARPA Surgical Competition asks teams to build robots that can close that gap by assisting human surgeons, performing surgical tasks on their own, and learning new tasks quickly. The goal is simple to state and hard to reach: treat more patients, faster, and better.

Our team is taking a distinctive path. We are putting dexterous robotic hands at the center of the work, so the robot can use the same instruments, in the same operating room, as the people beside it.

“We are building the first robotic hands that can perform surgical tasks as a human surgeon does, working directly with delicate tissue and using familiar surgical instruments.”
Nancy Pollard, team lead and professor, Carnegie Mellon Robotics Institute

Hands first, then everything a hand needs

A robotic hand is only as useful as what guides it. Our system pairs custom hands with the arms, eyes, judgment and safeguards to use them well in an operating room.

Hands built for surgery

Our soft, humanlike hands combine the gentle touch needed for tissue with the strength and dexterity needed for instruments. For this competition we are reinforcing them with an anatomical skeleton for lifting, pressing and pulling. An ordinary sterile glove slips over the hand, giving it a clean, disposable surface.

Current hands press with at least 20 newtons at the fingertips, with more at the palm. The complete hand and wrist, actuators included, will weigh 1 to 2 kilograms.

Two arms, one side of the bed

Two seven-jointed Kassow KR810 robot arms sit on a locked cart beside the bed, the same arms that run FuturHand’s factory systems today. Every robot base stays on one side, so the surgeon’s side remains open for access and a clear exit.

Each arm reaches 850 millimeters, carries 10 kilograms and repeats its position to within 0.03 millimeters.

Seeing the surgical field

3D cameras above the table capture the whole scene, while small cameras on each arm see around the hands and instruments. Together they track tool tips and tissue movement, recognize anatomical landmarks and sterile boundaries, and connect a pointing gesture to the thing being pointed at.

Taking direction like a teammate

The robot listens for spoken commands, watches for gestures, and works out what each one means in the scene in front of it. When something is unclear, it asks. A surgeon can hand off a task, or let the robot lead a series of approved steps while it checks its own results.

A surgeon says “Retract on that side and apply suction.” The robot has to link “that side” to the gesture, find the right tissue, hold it back with one hand and bring suction in with the other.

Speech, vision and planning all run on computers in the room, with no reliance on the cloud or an internet connection.

Learning a new skill in an hour

Many robot-learning methods need huge amounts of training data. Pollard’s team has developed a way to transfer skills to a robot in days instead. During the competition, teams get 60 minutes to teach a robot a task it has never seen. We will combine direct skill encoding, teleoperated demonstrations and imitation learning, and practice until the robot succeeds three times in a row on its own.

A person is always in charge

Autonomy is the goal, but a trained operator can pause the robot at any time and take direct control of both arms and hands using teleoperation tools from the University of Illinois. Switching back requires a deliberate enable step and a fresh safety check. A hardware emergency stop, separate from all software, halts every arm and hand, and in testing the hand keeps its grip when power is cut.

Safety is designed into the room

The robot’s place in the room is part of its safety plan. With every robot base on one side of the bed, the surgeon never has to cross the robot or wait for an arm to move out of the way.

The cart stays locked while the robot works, a designated observer watches clearances, and emergency stops sit within reach of the surgeon and every operator. Before any assessment, we rehearse full procedures on mannequins, including handoffs between autonomy and teleoperation, emergency stops and recovery.

Already working, from field medicine to the factory floor

We are not starting from scratch. Team members have put robots to work alongside medics, in live animal studies and in production factories, and those systems are the foundation for this one.

Nancy Pollard

Team lead. Professor, Robotics Institute, Carnegie Mellon University. Founder, FuturHand Robotics.

One of the foremost experts in dexterous manipulation, Pollard has spent more than two decades at CMU pioneering soft, highly dexterous robotic hands for fine manipulation. She founded FuturHand to bring research from her Dexterous Manipulation and Soft Robotics Lab into industry, where its robots now handle skilled work for Fortune 100 manufacturers.

From launch to final competition in six months

Most DARPA robotics challenges run for years. This one moves from launch to final scoring in a single season, so progress is fast and visible.

  1. September 2026

    DARPA launches the competition

    Teams begin preparing qualification submissions.

  2. November 13, 2026

    Qualification submitted

    Our technical plan, team and demonstration videos go to DARPA.

  3. November 2026

    Selected as one of 10 teams

    Altus Dexterity is chosen to compete.

  4. We are here

    Building the integrated system

    Bringing the arms, hands and operator interface together, with emergency stopping and controlled recovery.

  5. January 15–24, 2027

    First DARPA workshop

    In-person demonstration of the technology, integration testing and safety checks.

  6. March 20–26, 2027

    Final competition

    Scored trials of the robot working alongside surgeons, performing surgical tasks on its own, and learning new tasks quickly.

Teams compete for $3.5 million in prize money. Full rules and program details are on the DARPA Surgical Competition page.
